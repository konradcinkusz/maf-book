\chapter{Capstone}
\label{ch:capstone}

\begin{quote}\itshape
One system, no new API surface.
\end{quote}

\noindent
This chapter builds nothing new. Every mechanism it uses has appeared already ---
graphs from Chapter~\ref{ch:workflows-basics}, traces from
Chapter~\ref{ch:observability}, scoring from Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}, hosting
from Chapter~\ref{ch:hosting}. What it does is put them together into one system
that answers a question none of them answers alone.

That is the point of a capstone, and it is also the honest shape of most real
work in this area. The interesting systems are not the ones using an API nobody
else has found. They are the ones that connected things everybody had.

\begin{verifybox}
\textbf{This chapter is a design, not a shipped system.} The framework calls it
composes are verified and were established in earlier chapters; the composition
is the author's and has not been built as described. Read it as an architecture
with worked reasoning, and expect the schema decisions in
\S\ref{sec:cap-ingest} to be the ones you change first.
\end{verifybox}

% ============================================================
\section{The problem}
\label{sec:cap-problem}

Chapter~\ref{ch:observability} \S\ref{sec:obs-gap} put it in one line: the
framework instruments mechanism thoroughly and quality not at all, so a green
dashboard is compatible with an agent failing every user.

Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} closed half of that. You can score an agent, and you
can score a workflow run with a per-agent breakdown. But scoring happens in a
harness you run deliberately, against inputs you chose. It is a laboratory
instrument.

The gap that remains is the one between the two. Your traces know what happened
in production and not whether it was good. Your evaluations know what good looks
like and only for inputs you thought of. Nothing joins them --- and the questions
people actually ask live exactly in that join:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item This run scored badly. \emph{Which executor caused it?}
\item Group chat costs more than sequential here. \emph{Is it buying anything?}
\item We spend a lot of tokens on Tuesdays. \emph{On what?}
\item Quality has drifted down over six weeks. \emph{Starting when, and after
      what?}
\end{itemize}

None of those is answerable from traces alone or from scores alone. All of them
are answerable from traces \emph{with} scores attached. That join is the system.

\begin{note}
Chapter~\ref{ch:harness} \S\ref{sec:harness-tooling} arrived at the same place
from the other direction: the agent loop is now a free platform feature, and what
is not free is everything organisational. This chapter builds a piece of that
--- deliberately the piece that is useful at ten agents and indispensable at a
hundred.
\end{note}

\section{Architecture}
\label{sec:cap-architecture}

Four stages, and the discipline is that each is independently useful. A system
where stage four is the only thing that pays is a system that never ships.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}lX@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Stage} & \textbf{Does} \\
\midrule
Ingest & Receives OTLP spans, reconstructs runs, stores them \\[4pt]
Score & Replays stored runs through evaluators, attaches scores \\[4pt]
Compare & Aggregates scored runs by topology, model, version \\[4pt]
Surface & Finds runs worth a human's attention \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\noindent
Two decisions are worth making early because they are expensive to reverse.

\textbf{Store runs, not spans.} A span store is a tracing backend and you already
have one. What you need is a \emph{run} --- a reconstructed, queryable object with
its executors, turns, tokens and outcome --- because every question in
\S\ref{sec:cap-problem} is about runs. Reconstruct on ingest and store the
result.

\textbf{Score out of band.} Scoring is slow, costs model calls, and its
definition changes. If it happens on the ingest path, a scorer change means
re-ingesting and a scorer outage means losing data. Ingest fast, score later,
and keep enough of the run that re-scoring never needs the original traffic.

\needdiagram{capstone-architecture}{The four stages, and what crosses between
them}{A left-to-right pipeline: OTLP collector feeding an ingest service; ingest
writing reconstructed runs to a store; a separate scoring worker reading runs and
writing scores back against the same run identifiers; and a query layer over both
serving the comparison and waste views. Draw the scoring worker clearly off the
ingest path, with its own queue, because that separation is the architectural
point.}

\section{Ingesting GenAI traces}
\label{sec:cap-ingest}

This is the stage with the real engineering, and the one where earlier chapters
save you the most time --- because two facts from \S\ref{sec:obs-reading} would
otherwise cost you a rewrite.

\subsection{Subscribe to both sources}

Agents and workflows emit under two different activity source names. An ingest
that subscribes to one produces traces with a hole in them and no error to say
so. Chapter~\ref{ch:observability} gave both names; the collector configuration
must carry both, and this is worth an assertion at startup rather than a comment.

\subsection{Walk links, not parents}

Here is the fact that determines your schema. \textbf{Executor spans are
siblings, not children.} Causality between executors is carried by span
\emph{links}, created by propagating W3C trace context from the sending executor
and attaching a link on the receiving one.

So reconstructing a run means walking the link graph. An ingest written against
the usual parent-child assumption --- the assumption every trace-processing
tutorial makes --- produces a flat list of executor spans in arbitrary order,
with the causal structure silently discarded. It will look like it works.

\begin{warning}
This is the single most expensive mistake available in this chapter, because the
result is not an error but a plausible-looking view that is wrong. You will
discover it when a fan-out renders as a sequence and nobody can explain why the
order changes between runs.

Test it on a workflow with a fan-out and a fan-in, where the correct answer is
known and visibly different from the flat one.
\end{warning}

\subsection{What a run is}

The reconstruction produces one object per run. The fields worth having, and
where each comes from:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}lX@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Field} & \textbf{Source} \\
\midrule
Run and session identity & Workflow session span tags \\[4pt]
Topology & Workflow name and definition tag \\[4pt]
Executors & Executor spans, ordered by the link graph \\[4pt]
Turn count & Count of executor invocations \\[4pt]
Tokens & Usage content, summed, input and output separately \\[4pt]
Latency & Session span duration \\[4pt]
Dropped deliveries & Edge spans where delivery did not happen \\[4pt]
Outcome & Whether a workflow output span appeared \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\begin{note}
Capture dropped deliveries during ingest even though nothing downstream uses
them yet. They are nearly free to record, they are the signal from
\S\ref{sec:obs-dropped} that a graph is silently broken, and a fleet-wide count
of silent drops per topology is a genuinely novel number that nobody currently
has. It is the kind of thing that is trivial to record now and impossible to
recover later.
\end{note}

\subsection{Sampling and retention}

Two things to decide before this is running rather than after.

Chapter~\ref{ch:observability} argued against sampling agent runs at HTTP rates
--- runs are low-volume and expensive, and the rare failing one is the only
interesting one. Keep every run's reconstruction. What you can sample is the
detail: full message content is large, sensitive, and needed only for the runs
you will look at.

Which raises the same warning as \S\ref{sec:eval-set}, one layer up. This system
is a durable store of production conversation content, assembled by you, sitting
outside your application's normal data governance. Give it a retention policy and
an access model on the day it is built, not when someone asks.

\section{Scoring a workflow run}
\label{sec:cap-scoring}

The scorer is not the contribution here, and it is worth being clear about that
because it is where the temptation to over-build lives.
Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} established that scoring exists in the box: local
deterministic checks, judge-model evaluators, and a per-agent breakdown on
workflow runs.

The contribution is the \emph{join}. A score attached to a run whose executor
structure you reconstructed is a score you can attribute.

\begin{csharp}[caption={Scoring stored runs, out of band}, label={lst:cap-score}]
await foreach (StoredRun run in this._runs.UnscoredAsync(cancellationToken))
{
    AgentEvaluationResults results = await this._evaluator.EvaluateAsync(
        run.ToEvalItems(), evalName: run.Topology, cancellationToken);

    await this._scores.WriteAsync(new RunScore(
        RunId:      run.Id,
        Overall:    results.Passed / (double)results.Total,
        PerAgent:   results.SubResults?.ToDictionary(
                        kv => kv.Key,
                        kv => kv.Value.Passed / (double)kv.Value.Total),
        ScorerName: this._evaluator.Name,
        ScorerVersion: ScorerVersion), cancellationToken);
}
\end{csharp}

Two details in that listing matter more than they look.

\textbf{The per-agent dictionary is the whole point.} An overall score tells you a
run was bad. The breakdown tells you which participant was responsible, and
because the run object knows the executor structure, that participant can be
located in the graph.

\textbf{Scores are versioned by scorer.} Scorer definitions change --- a new
check, a different judge model --- and a score computed under one definition is
not comparable with a score computed under another. Store the version alongside
the score and never mix them in a chart. This is the same discipline
\S\ref{sec:eval-ci} demanded of gate thresholds, and forgetting it produces trend
lines with step changes that look like quality events and are measurement events.

\begin{note}
Start with the cheap tier only. A scoring worker running local checks over every
stored run costs nothing per run and will already answer most of
\S\ref{sec:cap-problem}. Add the judge tier for a sample once the pipeline is
proven, not before --- an unproven pipeline that also has a model bill is a
project that gets cancelled.
\end{note}

\section{Comparing topologies empirically}
\label{sec:cap-compare}

With scored runs in a store, the comparison Chapter~\ref{ch:orchestration}
specified stops being a one-off study and becomes a standing query.

That is a bigger difference than it sounds. \S\ref{sec:orch-benchmark}'s
benchmark is a controlled experiment: one fixed task, everything held constant,
twenty runs per pattern. Valuable, and expensive, and out of date the moment a
model changes. This system computes the same comparison continuously over real
traffic --- which is uncontrolled, and therefore answers a different and
complementary question.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}XX@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{The benchmark answers} & \textbf{This answers} \\
\midrule
Which pattern is better on this task, all else equal & Which pattern is better
on the work we actually get \\[4pt]
Under controlled conditions & Under production conditions, including the inputs
nobody designed for \\[4pt]
Once, expensively & Continuously, as a by-product \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\begin{warning}
Production comparison is confounded and the benchmark is not. If handoff handles
your hard tickets and sequential handles the easy ones, handoff will score worse
and be doing better work. Never read this view as a clean comparison --- it is
observational data, and the difference between topologies is entangled with the
difference between the work they were given.

Segment by task type before drawing any conclusion, and if you cannot segment,
say so on the chart rather than in a footnote nobody reads.
\end{warning}

The report worth building first is per topology: run count, median and spread of
turns, tokens and latency, success rate, and the per-agent score breakdown
averaged across runs. Medians and spreads rather than means, for the reasons
\S\ref{sec:orch-benchmark} gave --- a topology with a good median and a bad tail
is a topology that fails expensively and rarely, which is exactly what an
average hides.

\needscreenshot{capstone-topology-comparison}{Which topology, and why}{The
capstone's comparison view: several orchestration topologies as rows, with run
count, median turns, median tokens, median latency and success rate as columns,
and one row expanded to show the per-agent score breakdown underneath it. The
expanded row is the important part --- the reader must be able to see an
individual participant's contribution, not just the topology aggregate. Browser
only.}

\section{Surfacing wasted turns}
\label{sec:cap-waste}

The most immediately useful view, and the cheapest to build, because the signals
were already enumerated in \S\ref{sec:eval-set} for a different purpose. The
criteria that select good evaluation items are the criteria that select wasteful
runs --- which is not a coincidence, since both are looking for where the system
behaved unusually.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}lX@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Signal} & \textbf{Usually means} \\
\midrule
Turns far above median for its topology & A loop that did not converge \\[4pt]
Iteration or loop cap hit & Termination by timeout, not by completion \\[4pt]
High tokens, low score & Expensive failure --- the worst quadrant \\[4pt]
An agent that spoke and changed nothing & A participant earning nothing \\[4pt]
Dropped deliveries present & A silently broken graph \\[4pt]
Abandoned before output & A user who gave up \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\begin{note}
The fourth row is the one worth the effort. Chapter~\ref{ch:orchestration} raised
the case of the group-chat critic that contributes nothing --- and with per-agent
scores joined to run structure, that stops being an anecdote and becomes a
query: participants whose presence does not move the run's score, ranked by what
they cost. That number pays for the system on its own.
\end{note}

Rank by cost, not by count. A hundred slightly-wasteful runs matter less than
three runs that each burned a hundred thousand tokens arriving nowhere, and a
list sorted by frequency will show you the hundred.

\needscreenshot{capstone-wasted-turns}{Where the money went}{The waste view: runs
ranked by wasted cost, each row showing the topology, turn count against the
median for that topology, token cost, score, and the reason it was flagged. At
least one row expanded to show the run's executor sequence, with the point at
which it stopped making progress visible. Browser only.}

\section{Deploying it}
\label{sec:cap-deploy}

Nothing here is new either, which is the point of having read
Chapter~\ref{ch:hosting}.

The ingest is an ASP.NET Core service behind an OTLP endpoint. The scoring worker
is a background service reading a queue. Both need a database; runs and scores
are ordinary relational data and deserve no cleverness. Aspire wires the four
together locally, as in \S\ref{sec:host-aspire}.

Two properties deserve deliberate design.

\textbf{The ingest must not lose data when the scorer is down,} which is why the
stages are separate. Ingest writes runs; scoring is a queue consumer. A scorer
outage becomes a backlog, not a hole.

\textbf{Re-scoring must be cheap,} because scorer definitions change more often
than anything else in the system. If re-scoring the last ninety days requires
re-ingesting, the pipeline is wrong --- the stored run must contain everything a
scorer needs.

\begin{warning}
This system holds production conversation content for every run, and its whole
value is being easy to query. That combination is exactly what a data protection
review exists to catch, and it is better to meet that review with a retention
policy and an access model already in place than to meet it with a demo.

Apply Chapter~\ref{ch:hosting} \S\ref{sec:host-aspnet}'s isolation reasoning
here too: the people who may read this system's contents are not necessarily
everyone who can reach its URL.
\end{warning}

\section{What v1 deliberately leaves out}
\label{sec:cap-omissions}

Chapter~\ref{ch:harness} \S\ref{sec:harness-tooling} listed what the platform
does not give you and named this chapter as where it gets built. This section is
the honest accounting of how much of that list v1 actually addresses, which is
less than half.

\textbf{Built:} the fleet-level questions. Which topologies cost the most, which
fail most often, which participants earn nothing, where the waste is. That is the
part that only appears at a hundred agents across forty people, and it is the
part that was genuinely missing.

\textbf{Not built, and deliberately:}

\emph{Multi-user.} v1 assumes one team looking at their own agents. Tenancy,
per-team visibility and role-based access are real work and orthogonal to whether
the join is useful. Prove the join first.

\emph{An audit trail.} Recording what happened is not the same as producing
evidence a compliance reviewer will accept, which needs tamper-evidence, defined
retention and provenance for every score. That is a different product with a
different bar.

\emph{Policy enforcement.} This is the significant omission and it deserves
naming clearly. Chapter~\ref{ch:harness} observed that the framework gives good
defaults and a switch for each --- and that \emph{defaults are not policy}. There
is no way to say ``nobody in this organisation may turn the network on in a
shell sandbox'' and have that hold.

This system cannot fix that, because it observes rather than intercepts. It could
tell you the rule was broken after the fact, which is worth something and is not
enforcement. Real enforcement lives in the pipeline that builds the agent, not in
the system that watches it run --- and that is unsolved, genuinely valuable, and
the thing to build next.

\emph{Automated action.} v1 shows you the waste. It does not reroute traffic to a
cheaper topology or disable a participant that earns nothing. Acting on these
signals automatically requires confidence in them that nobody has yet, and the
right sequence is to measure for a quarter, check the recommendations against
judgement, and only then let it act.

\begin{note}
That last one is the discipline this whole book has been arguing for, applied to
itself. A system that acts on a measurement it has not validated is exactly the
green dashboard from \S\ref{sec:obs-gap}, one level up: confident, automated, and
wrong in a way nothing will report.
\end{note}

\section{Where to go next}
\label{sec:cap-next}

Two things this book owes and does not have, both stated plainly where they
belong and both worth more than another chapter of prose.

\textbf{The orchestration benchmark has not been run.}
\S\ref{sec:orch-benchmark} specifies it completely and reports nothing;
Appendix~\ref{app:providers}'s tables are empty and were kept empty. It needs a
budget and a few hours, not more research, and nobody has published it well for
1.0. Keep the raw event streams --- \S\ref{sec:cap-scoring} is the reason.

\textbf{The cold-start measurement has not been run either.}
\S\ref{sec:host-coldstart} specifies it, including the step that separates
platform cost from your own rehydration cost, which is the step that makes the
result worth publishing rather than restating a vendor's number.

Beyond those: policy enforcement in the build pipeline, from
\S\ref{sec:cap-omissions}. It is the clearest unsolved problem this book has
found, and unlike the two measurements it is not a matter of budget.

\subsection{A closing note on what changes}

Much of the API surface in this book will move. Names have already changed once
during its writing --- Chapter~\ref{ch:first-agent}'s vocabulary is not what the
early previews used --- and one activity source is still marked experimental.
Treat specific signatures as perishable and check them against the source, which
remains more authoritative than any documentation including this.

What will not move is the reasoning. That a superstep boundary is the only
consistent point to checkpoint. That a type mismatch on an edge is a silent drop
and needs a query rather than a debugger. That the topology is yours and the
routing is the model's. That a green dashboard is compatible with an agent
failing every user. That measurement beats assertion, and that a crude stable
scorer beats a sophisticated unstable one.

Those are properties of the problem, not of this framework. They will still be
true when the names have changed again.

\begin{exercisebox}
The last one:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item Build the ingest for one workflow. Confirm the fan-out renders as a
      fan-out. If it renders as a sequence, you walked parents.
\item Add the cheap scorer only, over stored runs. Resist the judge for a
      fortnight.
\item Compute the waste view. Find the most expensive pointless run in your own
      system. There is one.
\item Answer the participant question: is there an agent in your topology whose
      presence does not move the score? Cost it.
\item Run the benchmark in \S\ref{sec:orch-benchmark} and publish it. Of
      everything in this book, that is the piece the ecosystem is missing.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercisebox}

\section{Summary}

The capstone introduces no API. It joins two things the framework keeps apart:
traces, which know what happened and not whether it was good, and evaluation,
which knows what good looks like but only for inputs you chose. The join is where
every useful question lives.

Four stages, each independently useful: ingest reconstructs runs from spans,
scoring runs out of band over stored runs, comparison aggregates by topology, and
the waste view surfaces what deserves attention. Store runs rather than spans ---
you already have a span store --- and keep scoring off the ingest path so a
scorer change is a re-score rather than a re-ingest.

Ingest must subscribe to both activity sources and must walk span links rather
than parent-child nesting, because executor spans are siblings. The parent-child
version produces a plausible view that is wrong, which is worse than one that
fails.

The scorer is not the contribution; the attribution is. Per-agent breakdowns
joined to reconstructed run structure turn ``this run scored badly'' into ``this
participant caused it'', and version every score by the scorer that produced it
or your trend lines will show measurement changes as quality events.

Production comparison across topologies is confounded in a way the
Chapter~\ref{ch:orchestration} benchmark is not, and the two are complementary
rather than substitutes. Segment before concluding.

What v1 leaves out is most of the organisational layer: multi-user, audit, and
above all policy enforcement --- because this system observes rather than
intercepts, and telling you a rule was broken is not enforcing it. That gap is
the clearest unsolved problem in this book.

And the two measurements this book specifies and does not report are still
waiting. They are the most valuable thing left to do.
