\chapter{Workflows: Executors and Edges}
\label{ch:workflows-basics}

\begin{quote}\itshape
The chapter the rest of the book was waiting for.
\end{quote}

\noindent
Everything up to this point has been a single agent with tools. That is a
capable thing, and for a large class of problems it is the right thing. It is
also, architecturally, a loop: the model decides, calls something, looks at the
result, decides again. The control flow lives inside the model.

This chapter is about the case where you want the control flow to live in your
code. Not because the model is bad at deciding --- often it is fine --- but
because you need to know in advance what can happen, replay what did happen, run
two things at once, and put a person in the middle. None of that is expressible
in a loop whose next step is chosen by a language model.

The framework's answer is a directed graph of executors joined by edges. The
claim this chapter makes, and then defends, is that this is the genuinely
differentiated part of the Microsoft Agent Framework --- the part that is hard to
reproduce with a prompt and a for-loop, and the part most worth your time.

\section{Why a graph and not a chain}
\label{sec:wf-why-graph}
\index{workflow!graph model}

The obvious design for multi-step agent work is a chain: do this, then that, then
the other. Several frameworks offer exactly that and it reads beautifully in a
README. It fails on four things, and each failure is a structural one rather than
a missing feature.

\textbf{Branches.} A chain has one successor. Real processes have several, chosen
at runtime --- the spam classifier that routes three ways, the triage step that
picks a specialist. You can fake it with an \code{if} inside a step, but then the
branch is invisible to the runtime: it cannot be drawn, validated, traced, or
resumed at the branch point.

\textbf{Concurrency.} Two independent analyses of the same document should run at
once. In a chain they cannot, because a chain's only ordering primitive is
sequence.

\textbf{Joins.} Once you have concurrency you need somewhere for the results to
meet, with a defined synchronisation rule. That is a graph construct --- a
barrier --- and it has no chain equivalent.

\textbf{Interruption.} A run that pauses for human approval and resumes an hour
later in a different process needs its position to be a value you can serialise.
``Where are we in the chain'' is a program counter; ``which nodes have pending
messages'' is data. Only the second survives a process restart. This is
Chapter~\ref{ch:workflows-advanced}'s subject, and the graph model is what makes
it possible at all.

\subsection{Supersteps}
\label{sec:wf-supersteps}
\index{superstep}
\index{SuperStepCompletedEvent@\texttt{SuperStepCompletedEvent}}
\index{workflow!execution model}
\index{workflow!superstep}

The execution model is not ``walk the graph''. It is a sequence of
\emph{supersteps}, which is worth internalising early because it explains most of
the framework's otherwise-surprising behaviour.

A superstep proceeds in three phases:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item Every executor with pending messages is delivered its messages and runs.
      Within a superstep these runs are independent and may be concurrent.
\item Messages emitted during the superstep are buffered. They are \emph{not}
      delivered to their targets in the same superstep.
\item The superstep ends. Buffered messages become the pending messages of the
      next superstep, queued state updates are applied, and --- if checkpointing
      is configured --- a checkpoint may be taken.
\end{enumerate}

The runtime announces each boundary. Two events carry the step number:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,nosep]
\item \api{SuperStepStartedEvent}
\item \api{SuperStepCompletedEvent}
\end{itemize}

\medskip
\noindent
The completion event's payload reports which executors were activated, which were
instantiated for the first time, and whether anything remains pending:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}lX@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Member} & \textbf{Meaning} \\
\midrule
\code{ActivatedExecutors} & Executors that ran during the step \\
\code{InstantiatedExecutors} & Executors constructed for the first time \\
\code{StateUpdated} & Whether queued state updates were applied \\
\code{HasPendingMessages} & Whether another step will follow \\
\code{HasPendingRequests} & Whether the run is blocked on external input \\
\code{Checkpoint} & The checkpoint taken at this boundary, if any \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\begin{note}
The superstep boundary is the unit of durability, the unit of concurrency, and
the unit of observability all at once. When Chapter~\ref{ch:workflows-advanced}
resumes a workflow from a checkpoint, it resumes at a superstep boundary --- not
mid-executor. That is why a partially completed executor cannot be resumed and
why executors should be written to be re-runnable.
\end{note}

The practical consequence for this chapter: \emph{a message you send is not
visible to its target until the next superstep}. Code that sends a message and
then expects to observe its effect is wrong, and it is wrong in a way that looks
like a race condition rather than a design error.

\section{Executors}
\label{sec:wf-executors}
\index{Executor<TIn,TOut>@\texttt{Executor<TIn,TOut>}}
\index{executor!base types}
\index{HandleAsync@\texttt{HandleAsync}}
\index{Executor@\texttt{Executor}}

An executor is the unit of work: something that receives a typed message, does
something, and optionally produces a typed result. The base type is
\api{Executor}, and it has two generic conveniences that cover almost everything
you will write.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}lX@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Base type} & \textbf{Use when} \\
\midrule
\code{Executor} & The executor handles more than one input type, or configures
its own routing \\[4pt]
\code{Executor<TIn>} & One input type, no return value --- side effects and
explicit sends only \\[4pt]
\code{Executor<TIn,TOut>} & One input type, one output type; the return value is
sent along outgoing edges \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

The two-parameter form is the one to reach for. Its handler signature is the
whole contract:

\begin{csharp}[caption={The smallest useful executor}, label={lst:wf-executor-basic}]
internal sealed class UppercaseExecutor() : Executor<string, string>("Uppercase")
{
    public override ValueTask<string> HandleAsync(
        string message,
        IWorkflowContext context,
        CancellationToken cancellationToken = default)
    {
        // The return value is sent as a message along every outgoing edge.
        return ValueTask.FromResult(message.ToUpperInvariant());
    }
}
\end{csharp}

Three things in that signature deserve comment. The identifier passed to the base
constructor is the executor's identity in the graph --- it appears in every event,
every trace, and every checkpoint, so name it as you would name a log source, not
as you would name a variable. The \api{IWorkflowContext} is the only sanctioned
route to the outside: events, messages, state, and halting all go through it. And
the return value is not a return value in the ordinary sense --- it is a message
emission, which means an executor that returns a value it did not intend to
broadcast has just introduced a bug that the type system will not catch.

\subsection{The context}
\label{sec:wf-context}
\index{QueueStateUpdateAsync@\texttt{QueueStateUpdateAsync}}
\index{workflow!state}
\index{state!scoped}
\index{IWorkflowContext@\texttt{IWorkflowContext}}

\api{IWorkflowContext} is small, which is a good sign. The members you will use
constantly:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}lX@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Member} & \textbf{Purpose} \\
\midrule
\code{SendMessageAsync} & Emit a message, optionally to a named target \\
\code{YieldOutputAsync} & Emit a workflow output without ending the run \\
\code{AddEventAsync} & Raise a custom event onto the stream \\
\code{QueueStateUpdateAsync} & Write scoped state, applied at step end \\
\code{ReadStateAsync} & Read scoped state \\
\code{RequestHaltAsync} & Ask the runtime to stop \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

Note the word \emph{queue} in the state-writing method. State updates are applied
at the superstep boundary, not immediately, for the same reason messages are:
so that the boundary is a consistent point to checkpoint. Reading back a value
you queued in the same superstep is not guaranteed to give you the new one.

\begin{warning}
State written through \api{QueueStateUpdateAsync} is captured by checkpointing.
State written to an instance field on your executor is not, unless you handle
\code{OnCheckpointingAsync} yourself. This is the single most common way to build
a workflow that resumes into a subtly wrong state, and
Chapter~\ref{ch:workflows-advanced} is largely about it. The rule for this
chapter: \emph{if it matters after a restart, it goes in the context}.
\end{warning}

\subsection{A function is an executor}
\label{sec:wf-function-executor}
\index{ExecutorBinding@\texttt{ExecutorBinding}}
\index{FunctionExecutor@\texttt{FunctionExecutor}}
\index{executor!placeholder}
\index{executor!as function}
\index{testing!workflow seam}

The symmetry the chapter opened with is not a metaphor. It is implemented as a
set of implicit conversions on \api{ExecutorBinding}, the type every builder
method actually accepts:

\begin{csharp}[caption={What can be a node}, label={lst:wf-bindings}]
// All four of these are implicitly convertible to ExecutorBinding,
// so all four can be passed to AddEdge:

Executor  custom = new UppercaseExecutor();   // a class you wrote
AIAgent   agent  = chatClient.AsAIAgent(...); // an agent
RequestPort port = ...;                       // a human-in-the-loop port
string    later  = "Summariser";              // a placeholder, bound later
\end{csharp}

An agent becomes a node because \api{AIAgent} converts to a binding. A plain
function becomes a node through \api{FunctionExecutor}, which wraps a delegate
with the same handler shape. A string becomes a \emph{placeholder} --- a node
declared now and bound to an implementation later, which is what makes
dependency-injected and declaratively loaded graphs possible.

That is the whole idea, and it has a practical payoff worth stating plainly:
\textbf{you can replace an agent with a function, or a function with an agent,
without touching the graph}. When a step turns out to be deterministic, delete
the model call. When a deterministic step turns out to need judgement, promote it.
The edges do not change.

\begin{note}
This is also the cheapest test-seam in the framework. A workflow whose model
calls are agents is untestable without a model; the same workflow with those
nodes bound to stub functions runs in a unit test in milliseconds. Design the
graph so that swap is a one-line change --- placeholders make it one.
\end{note}

\section{Typed messages}
\label{sec:wf-messages}
\index{workflow!message typing}

Edges carry typed messages, and the routing is by \emph{runtime} type. An
executor declares which types it can handle; the edge runner asks the target
whether it can handle the message's actual type, and delivers only if it can.

This is where the first real gotcha lives, and it is worth the space.

\subsection{What happens on a type mismatch}
\label{sec:wf-mismatch}
\index{workflow!silent drop}
\index{delivery status}
\index{workflow!type mismatch}
\index{edge!dropped delivery}

Nothing. That is the answer, and it is not the answer most engineers expect.

If a message arrives at an edge whose target cannot handle its type, the message
is \emph{silently dropped}. No exception, no warning event, no failed executor.
The run continues, the downstream node simply never fires, and the workflow
either produces no output or produces a partial one. The only evidence is a
telemetry tag: the edge delivery span is tagged with a delivery status, whose
values include the following.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}lX@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Status} & \textbf{Meaning} \\
\midrule
\code{delivered} & The target accepted the message type \\
\code{dropped type mismatch} & The target cannot handle this runtime type \\
\code{dropped target mismatch} & The message was addressed to a different target \\
\code{dropped condition false} & The edge's condition returned false \\
\code{buffered} & Held at a barrier, awaiting other inputs \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\begin{warning}
\textbf{A workflow that produces no output is more often a type mismatch than a
logic error.} The debugging procedure is: turn on the workflow activity source,
run it, and look for edge spans whose delivery status is not \code{delivered}.
Chapter~\ref{ch:observability} wires this up properly; until then, a breakpoint in
each executor's handler tells you which node never fired, and the edge into it is
the one to inspect.
\end{warning}

Why does the builder not catch this at build time? Because it genuinely cannot,
and the framework's own source is unusually candid about why. Four reasons, in
the order they bite:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item Executors are not required to declare their produced types exhaustively, so
      the set of types flowing out of a node is not statically known.
\item Conditional edges and target selectors route at runtime by value, not by
      type.
\item Fan-in edges are intended to grow aggregation strategies that transform
      types on the way through.
\item Executors may be created by asynchronous factories and register their
      handlers at construction, so their accepted types are not knowable until
      they are instantiated --- and \code{Build()} is synchronous, because it must
      be, to work with dependency injection.
\end{itemize}

The last one is the real blocker and it is an honest trade: synchronous,
DI-compatible construction was worth more than build-time type validation. Knowing
that is what tells you where to spend your own effort --- which is
\S\ref{sec:wf-generators}.

\subsection{Exact types, not assignable ones}

Routing matches the runtime type exactly. An executor whose input type is
declared as an interface will \emph{not} receive an implementation of it --- a
node expecting \code{IEnumerable<ChatMessage>} never sees a
\code{List<ChatMessage>}, even though the assignment would compile.

This matters immediately, because the agent host node emits its response messages
as a \code{List<ChatMessage>}. An executor downstream of an agent must declare
that type, not an interface over it:

\begin{csharp}[caption={Declaring the type an agent actually sends}, label={lst:wf-agent-downstream}]
// Correct: matches the runtime type the agent host emits.
internal sealed class FinalOutputExecutor()
    : Executor<List<ChatMessage>, string>("FinalOutput")
{
    public override ValueTask<string> HandleAsync(
        List<ChatMessage> message,
        IWorkflowContext context,
        CancellationToken cancellationToken = default)
    {
        string combined = string.Join("\n", message.Select(m => m.Text ?? "")).Trim();
        return ValueTask.FromResult(combined);
    }
}
\end{csharp}

\subsection{The chat protocol}
\label{sec:wf-chat-protocol}
\index{TurnToken@\texttt{TurnToken}}
\index{chat protocol}
\index{executor!adapter}
\index{SendsMessage@\texttt{SendsMessage}}
\index{workflow!chat protocol}

Agents in a workflow do not run when they receive a message. They accumulate
messages, and run when they receive a \api{TurnToken}. This is deliberate --- it
is what lets several upstream nodes contribute to one agent turn --- but it means
connecting a plain executor to an agent requires an adapter that does two things:

\begin{csharp}[caption={Adapting a plain type to the chat protocol}, label={lst:wf-adapter}]
[SendsMessage(typeof(ChatMessage))]
[SendsMessage(typeof(TurnToken))]
internal sealed class StringToChatMessageExecutor(string id) : Executor<string>(id)
{
    public override async ValueTask HandleAsync(
        string message,
        IWorkflowContext context,
        CancellationToken cancellationToken = default)
    {
        // 1. The content the agent should see.
        await context.SendMessageAsync(
            new ChatMessage(ChatRole.User, message),
            cancellationToken: cancellationToken);

        // 2. The signal to take a turn. Without this the agent never runs.
        await context.SendMessageAsync(
            new TurnToken(emitEvents: true),
            cancellationToken: cancellationToken);
    }
}
\end{csharp}

The \code{SendsMessage} attributes declare types this executor emits through the
context rather than through its return value. They are not decoration: they feed
the protocol descriptor the runtime uses for validation and visualisation, and
omitting them is how a graph comes to be drawn wrong.

\begin{note}
\textbf{Adapter nodes are the most common thing you will write and the most
common thing missing from a broken workflow.} Whenever an edge crosses between
plain types and the chat protocol --- in either direction --- there is an adapter
on it. If a workflow mixing executors and agents hangs, look for a missing
\api{TurnToken} before looking anywhere else.
\end{note}

\section{Edges}
\label{sec:wf-edges}
\index{edge!direct}
\index{edge!conditional}
\index{edge!fan-out}
\index{edge!fan-in barrier}
\index{SwitchBuilder@\texttt{SwitchBuilder}}
\index{AddChain@\texttt{AddChain}}
\index{concurrency!fan-out}
\index{workflow!edges}

Four kinds, and they compose.

\subsection{Direct}

The default. One source, one target, everything the source emits that the target
can handle.

\begin{csharp}[caption={A direct edge}, label={lst:wf-edge-direct}]
builder.AddEdge(analyse, summarise);
\end{csharp}

There is also \api{AddChain}, which takes a list and wires it end to end --- a
convenience worth using for the linear stretches that most real graphs contain.

\subsection{Conditional}

A generic overload takes a predicate over the message. The edge delivers only
when the predicate holds; otherwise the delivery is dropped with the
\code{dropped condition false} status.

\begin{csharp}[caption={A conditional edge}, label={lst:wf-edge-conditional}]
builder.AddEdge<AnalysisResult>(
    analyse,
    archive,
    condition: result => result?.Length <= LongDocumentThreshold);
\end{csharp}

For more than two outcomes, \api{AddSwitch} reads better than a stack of
conditions and makes the default case explicit:

\begin{csharp}[caption={Switch-case routing}, label={lst:wf-edge-switch}]
builder.AddSwitch(classify, sw => sw
    .AddCase<Classification>(c => c?.Decision == Decision.Clean,   assistant)
    .AddCase<Classification>(c => c?.Decision == Decision.Spam,    handleSpam)
    .WithDefault(handleUncertain));
\end{csharp}

\begin{warning}
A switch without \code{WithDefault} is a switch that silently drops anything no
case matched --- the same failure mode as a type mismatch, with the same absence
of diagnostics. Always write the default, even when you believe it is
unreachable. Especially then: the value being switched on usually came from a
model.
\end{warning}

\subsection{Fan-out}

One source, several targets. In its simplest form every target gets the message;
with a target selector, the source chooses which subset by index:

\begin{csharp}[caption={Fan-out, unconditional and selective}, label={lst:wf-edge-fanout}]
// Everyone gets it, and the two run concurrently.
builder.AddFanOutEdge(start, [physicist, chemist]);

// Or choose targets per message: (message, targetCount) => indices.
builder.AddFanOutEdge<AnalysisResult>(
    analyse,
    [handleSpam, assistant, summarise, handleUncertain],
    targetSelector: (result, count) => SelectTargets(result, count));
\end{csharp}

Fan-out is how concurrency is expressed. The targets run in the same superstep;
there is no ordering between them and you must not write code that assumes one.

\subsection{Fan-in and aggregation}

Several sources, one target, with a barrier: the target does not run until every
declared source has delivered.

\begin{csharp}[caption={A barrier join}, label={lst:wf-edge-fanin}]
builder.AddFanInBarrierEdge([physicist, chemist], aggregate);
\end{csharp}

The aggregating node receives the collected messages. Two properties of the
barrier are worth knowing before you rely on it: it filters by type like any
other edge, so a source whose message the target cannot handle contributes
nothing and the barrier may never release; and the barrier is per-run, not
per-message, so a graph with a cycle through a barrier needs care.

\needdiagram{wf-edge-kinds}{The four edge kinds, and what each one does at a
superstep boundary}{Four small graphs side by side: direct (A->B); conditional
(A->B labelled with a predicate, A->C for the else); fan-out (A->{B,C} with both
in one superstep band); fan-in barrier ({B,C}->D with D shown in the following
superstep band). Shade superstep bands so the timing difference between fan-out
and fan-in is visible.}

\section{\texorpdfstring{\api{WorkflowBuilder}}{WorkflowBuilder}}
\label{sec:wf-builder}
\index{InProcessExecution@\texttt{InProcessExecution}}
\index{execution mode!Lockstep}
\index{workflow!reachability validation}
\index{WithOutputFrom@\texttt{WithOutputFrom}}
\index{testing!Lockstep}
\index{WorkflowBuilder@\texttt{WorkflowBuilder}}

The builder is deliberately thin. It takes a start node in its constructor,
accumulates edges, and validates on \code{Build()}.

\begin{csharp}[caption={Building and running a graph}, label={lst:wf-builder}]
WorkflowBuilder builder = new(uppercase);

Workflow workflow = builder
    .AddEdge(uppercase, reverse)
    .WithOutputFrom(reverse)
    .WithName("text-pipeline")
    .Build();

await using StreamingRun run =
    await InProcessExecution.RunStreamingAsync(workflow, input: "Hello, World!");
\end{csharp}

\api{WithOutputFrom} marks which nodes' emissions count as workflow output. It is
not optional in practice: a graph with no output nodes runs to completion and
tells you nothing.

\subsection{What Build validates}

One thing, and it is worth knowing exactly which: \textbf{reachability}. Every
bound executor must be reachable from the start node, or \code{Build()} throws
with the offending identifiers listed. Placeholders must have been bound. Nothing
else is checked --- in particular, and as \S\ref{sec:wf-mismatch} explained, no
type compatibility along edges.

The check can be turned off with \code{Build(validateOrphans: false)}. Resist
this. An unreachable executor is almost always a missing edge, and the exception
naming it is the most useful diagnostic the builder produces.

\subsection{Running}

Three axes: streaming or not, and which execution mode.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}lX@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Entry point} & \textbf{Gives you} \\
\midrule
\code{RunAsync} & A completed \api{Run}; events collected, inspected afterwards \\
\code{RunStreamingAsync} & A \api{StreamingRun}; events as they happen \\
\code{OpenStreamingAsync} & A run with no initial input, fed by you \\
\code{ResumeAsync} & A run continued from a checkpoint (Ch.~\ref{ch:workflows-advanced}) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\noindent
The execution environment is chosen by static property on
\api{InProcessExecution}: \code{OffThread} (the default), \code{Lockstep}, and
\code{Concurrent}. \code{Lockstep} is the one to know about --- it makes superstep
boundaries deterministic, which is what you want in tests.

\begin{note}
Use \code{Lockstep} for every workflow unit test you write. The default
off-thread mode is right for production and wrong for assertions: a test that
passes under concurrency but asserts on ordering is a test that will fail in CI
on a busier machine, and you will spend an afternoon on it.
\end{note}

\section{Compile-time route validation}
\label{sec:wf-generators}
\index{MessageHandler@\texttt{MessageHandler}}
\index{diagnostics!MAFGENWF}
\index{source generators}
\index{analyzer reference}
\index{workflow!source generators}
\index{Workflows.Generators@\texttt{Workflows.Generators}}

This section covers the part of the framework nobody writes about, and it is the
direct answer to \S\ref{sec:wf-mismatch}: if the builder cannot validate routing
at build time, move the validation into the compiler.

\pkg{Microsoft.Agents.AI.Workflows.Generators} is a Roslyn source generator. You
mark handler methods with \code{[MessageHandler]}, declare the class
\code{partial}, and the generator writes the routing configuration that you would
otherwise write by hand --- and, more importantly, emits compiler diagnostics when
your handlers do not have a shape the runtime can route.

\begin{csharp}[caption={Multiple handlers, generated routing}, label={lst:wf-message-handler}]
internal sealed partial class WriterExecutor : Executor
{
    private readonly AIAgent _agent;

    public WriterExecutor(AIAgent agent) : base("Writer") => this._agent = agent;

    [MessageHandler]
    public async ValueTask<ChatMessage> HandleInitialRequestAsync(
        string message, IWorkflowContext context, CancellationToken ct = default)
        => await this.WriteAsync(new ChatMessage(ChatRole.User, message), ct);

    [MessageHandler]
    public async ValueTask<ChatMessage> HandleRevisionRequestAsync(
        CriticDecision decision, IWorkflowContext context, CancellationToken ct = default)
        => await this.WriteAsync(BuildRevisionPrompt(decision), ct);
}
\end{csharp}

Two handlers, two input types, one node. Without the generator this is a manual
\code{ConfigureProtocol} override registering both routes; with it, the routes are
derived from the signatures and cannot drift from them.

\subsection{The diagnostics}

Seven, and they are the reason to use this rather than the generated boilerplate.
Each is a build error or warning at the offending method, in the editor, before
anything runs.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}lX@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{ID} & \textbf{Condition} \\
\midrule
\code{MAFGENWF001} & Handler is missing its \api{IWorkflowContext} second parameter \\
\code{MAFGENWF002} & Handler returns something other than void, \code{ValueTask}, or \code{ValueTask<T>} \\
\code{MAFGENWF003} & Class has handlers but is not declared \code{partial} \\
\code{MAFGENWF004} & Handler is on a class that does not derive from \api{Executor} \\
\code{MAFGENWF005} & Handler has fewer than two parameters \\
\code{MAFGENWF006} & Class already defines \code{ConfigureProtocol}; handlers ignored \\
\code{MAFGENWF007} & Handler is \code{static} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\code{MAFGENWF006} is the interesting one. It is a warning rather than an error,
and it catches the case where someone adds a hand-written protocol override to a
class that already had generated routes --- at which point the generated ones stop
being used and half the node's handlers silently stop receiving messages. That is
precisely the failure from \S\ref{sec:wf-mismatch}, caught at compile time
instead of found in production.

\subsection{Referencing it}

The generator ships as a separate package marked as a development dependency, and
is referenced as an analyzer rather than as a library:

\begin{xmlcode}[caption={Adding the generator}, label={lst:wf-generator-ref}]
<PackageReference Include="Microsoft.Agents.AI.Workflows.Generators"
                  PrivateAssets="all" />
\end{xmlcode}

\begin{verifybox}
The upstream samples add the generator as an explicit project reference with
\code{OutputItemType="Analyzer"} even though the Workflows project itself
references it, which suggests the analyzer does not flow transitively to package
consumers and must be referenced directly. The author has not confirmed this from
a clean \code{dotnet new} project against the published packages. If
\code{[MessageHandler]} compiles but produces no routing, this reference is the
first thing to check.
\end{verifybox}

\begin{note}
The generator targets \code{netstandard2.0} against Roslyn 4.4, deliberately, so
that it works on older SDKs. That is a compatibility decision with a cost: it
cannot use newer analyzer APIs. It is mentioned here only because it explains why
the generator is a separate package rather than folded into the main one.
\end{note}

\section{Streaming workflow events}
\label{sec:wf-events}
\index{WorkflowEvent@\texttt{WorkflowEvent}}
\index{ToMermaidString@\texttt{ToMermaidString}}
\index{ExecutorFailedEvent@\texttt{ExecutorFailedEvent}}
\index{Graphviz}
\index{workflow!events}

A run is observed as an asynchronous stream of \api{WorkflowEvent}. The hierarchy
is shallow and the useful members are these.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}lX@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Event} & \textbf{Raised when} \\
\midrule
\code{WorkflowStartedEvent} & The run begins \\
\code{SuperStepStartedEvent} & A superstep opens \\
\code{ExecutorInvokedEvent} & An executor is handed a message \\
\code{ExecutorCompletedEvent} & An executor returns; carries its result \\
\code{ExecutorFailedEvent} & An executor threw; carries the exception \\
\code{AgentResponseUpdateEvent} & A token-level update from an agent node \\
\code{AgentResponseEvent} & A complete agent response \\
\code{SuperStepCompletedEvent} & A superstep closes; carries the step summary \\
\code{RequestInfoEvent} & The run is blocked on external input \\
\code{WorkflowOutputEvent} & A node marked as output emitted something \\
\code{WorkflowWarningEvent} & A non-fatal problem \\
\code{WorkflowErrorEvent} & The run failed \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\begin{csharp}[caption={Watching a run}, label={lst:wf-watch}]
await using StreamingRun run =
    await InProcessExecution.RunStreamingAsync(workflow, input);

await foreach (WorkflowEvent evt in run.WatchStreamAsync())
{
    switch (evt)
    {
        case ExecutorCompletedEvent done:
            Console.WriteLine($"{done.ExecutorId}: {done.Data}");
            break;

        case ExecutorFailedEvent failed:
            Console.Error.WriteLine($"{failed.ExecutorId} failed: {failed.Data}");
            break;

        case WorkflowOutputEvent output:
            Console.WriteLine($"OUTPUT: {output}");
            break;

        case WorkflowErrorEvent error:
            Console.Error.WriteLine(error.Exception?.ToString());
            break;
    }
}
\end{csharp}

Note that \api{AgentResponseUpdateEvent} and \api{AgentResponseEvent} both derive
from \api{WorkflowOutputEvent}. A \code{case WorkflowOutputEvent} arm placed
above them in a switch will swallow every agent token, which produces a run that
appears to emit enormous amounts of output and no result. Order the arms
most-derived first.

\subsection{Drawing the graph}
\index{workflow!visualisation}

A built workflow can render itself, which is the fastest way to check that the
graph you described is the graph you meant:

\begin{csharp}[caption={Rendering the graph}, label={lst:wf-visualise}]
string mermaid = workflow.ToMermaidString();
string dot     = workflow.ToDotString();
\end{csharp}

Paste the Mermaid output into any Markdown renderer, or feed the DOT to
Graphviz. Do this once per workflow before running it. A missing edge is obvious
in a picture and invisible in a builder chain.

\section{Worked example: a document pipeline}
\label{sec:wf-pipeline}

The example is built as a ladder. Each rung is runnable, and each adds exactly
one concept, so that when a rung breaks you know which concept broke it.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item \textbf{Two functions in sequence.} No agents, no models. Confirms the
      build, the run, and the event stream. If this does not work nothing else
      will, and it fails in seconds rather than after a model call.
\item \textbf{Add an agent as the middle node.} The graph does not change shape.
      What changes is that an adapter is now required on both sides of the agent
      --- \S\ref{sec:wf-chat-protocol}. This rung is where most people first meet
      the missing-\api{TurnToken} hang.
\item \textbf{Add a conditional edge.} Route on a classification produced by the
      agent. Write the default case. Then deliberately break the predicate and
      watch the workflow produce nothing at all, so that the silent-drop
      behaviour of \S\ref{sec:wf-mismatch} is something you have seen rather than
      read about.
\item \textbf{Fan out and join.} Two independent analysers on the same document,
      a barrier, and an aggregator. Time this rung against rung three: the
      difference is the point of the whole chapter.
\item \textbf{Convert the executors to \code{[MessageHandler]} and turn on the
      generator.} No behaviour change. Then remove a \code{partial} keyword and
      confirm \code{MAFGENWF003}.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
The finished graph is six nodes: ingest, classify (agent), a fan-out to two
analysers (agent and function), a barrier into an aggregator, and a formatter
marked as the output node.

\begin{verifybox}
The pipeline listing below is assembled from patterns verified individually
against the upstream samples, but the composite has not been compiled or run by
the author. Treat it as a shape to type in rather than code to paste.
\end{verifybox}

\begin{csharp}[caption={The finished pipeline}, label={lst:wf-pipeline}]
IngestExecutor    ingest    = new();
ClassifyAdapter   toChat    = new("ToChat");
AIAgent           classifier = chatClient.AsAIAgent(ClassifierOptions);
ClassifyResult    fromChat  = new("FromChat");
AIAgent           riskAgent = chatClient.AsAIAgent(RiskOptions);
StatisticsExecutor stats    = new();
AggregateExecutor aggregate = new();
FormatExecutor    format    = new();

Workflow workflow = new WorkflowBuilder(ingest)
    .AddChain(ingest, [toChat, classifier, fromChat])
    .AddFanOutEdge(fromChat, [riskAgent, stats])
    .AddFanInBarrierEdge([riskAgent, stats], aggregate)
    .AddEdge(aggregate, format)
    .WithOutputFrom(format)
    .WithName("document-pipeline")
    .Build();
\end{csharp}

\begin{exercisebox}
Before Chapter~\ref{ch:workflows-advanced}:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item Build rungs one and two. Then remove the \api{TurnToken} send from the
      adapter and record exactly what the run does. This is the failure you will
      meet most often; meet it deliberately once.
\item Break an edge's types on purpose --- have a node emit \code{string} where
      the target expects a record. Confirm that nothing is thrown and nothing is
      logged. Then find the dropped delivery in the trace.
\item Time rung three against rung four on the same document, ten runs each.
      Report the median and the spread, not the best run. Appendix~\ref{app:providers}
      has the table to fill in.
\item Replace one agent node with a stub function binding and run the whole
      pipeline with no model at all. Measure how long the test takes. Decide
      whether your production graph is testable.
\item Render \code{ToMermaidString()} for your graph before and after adding the
      fan-in. Keep both. It is the cheapest documentation you will produce.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercisebox}

\section{Summary}

A workflow is a directed graph of executors joined by typed edges, executed as a
sequence of supersteps. The superstep boundary is simultaneously the unit of
concurrency, the unit of durability, and the unit of observability --- which is
why messages sent during a step are not delivered until the next one, and why
state must be queued through the context rather than written to a field.

An executor is anything that handles a typed message. A class you wrote, an
agent, a plain function and a not-yet-bound placeholder are all convertible to
the same binding type, which means a node's implementation can be swapped without
touching the graph. That symmetry is the framework's central idea and its best
test seam.

Routing is by exact runtime type, and a message whose target cannot handle its
type is dropped silently --- no exception, no event, only a telemetry tag. The
builder validates reachability and nothing else, for four reasons the framework's
own source sets out honestly, of which the binding one is that DI-compatible
synchronous construction was worth more than build-time type checking.

The generator package closes that gap: \code{[MessageHandler]} on a
\code{partial} executor, routes derived from signatures, and seven compiler
diagnostics that catch at build time the class of error that otherwise appears as
a workflow producing no output. It is the least documented and most useful thing
in this part of the framework.

Four edge kinds cover the shapes: direct, conditional, fan-out for concurrency,
fan-in barrier for joins. Always write the default case on a switch. Always mark
output nodes. Always draw the graph before running it.

Chapter~\ref{ch:workflows-advanced} takes the same graph and kills the process
half way through.
