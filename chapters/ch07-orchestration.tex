\chapter{Multi-Agent Orchestration}
\label{ch:orchestration}

\begin{quote}\itshape
Five patterns, one benchmark, and the question of when to abandon all five.
\end{quote}

\noindent
This is the chapter everyone wants to read first, and it is deliberately placed
after the two that explain how the machinery works. The reason is that the
orchestration patterns are not a separate system. They are small builders that
emit the graphs of Chapter~\ref{ch:workflows-basics}, and the moment one of them
stops fitting your problem --- which will happen --- the only useful thing to
know is what it was building on your behalf.

So this chapter does two things the marketing material does not. It shows the
patterns as the graph constructions they are, from the source. And it sets out a
measurement harness for choosing between them, because the question ``which
orchestration pattern should I use'' currently gets answered with adjectives.

\begin{verifybox}
Derived from the source of \pkg{Microsoft.Agents.AI.Workflows} --- the
orchestration builders and the \code{Specialized/} folder --- together with the
orchestration samples upstream. Type and member names are reliable. The benchmark
harness in \S\ref{sec:orch-benchmark} is the author's own design and has not been
run; \textbf{no results are reported in this chapter}, and \S\ref{sec:orch-benchmark}
says so explicitly rather than offering plausible numbers.
\end{verifybox}

\section{Patterns are conveniences over the graph}
\label{sec:orch-conveniences}
\index{orchestration!as graph construction}

The claim is easy to state and usually asserted without evidence, so here is the
evidence. Five builders sit in the same folder as \api{WorkflowBuilder}, and all
five derive from a common base:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}lX@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Builder} & \textbf{Pattern} \\
\midrule
\code{SequentialWorkflowBuilder} & Agents in a chain \\
\code{ConcurrentWorkflowBuilder} & Agents in parallel, results aggregated \\
\code{GroupChatWorkflowBuilder} & Turn-taking under a manager \\
\code{HandoffWorkflowBuilder} & Agents transfer control to one another \\
\code{MagenticWorkflowBuilder} & A manager plans, delegates and replans \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\noindent
Each has a \code{Build()} method, and each of those methods constructs an
ordinary workflow. The sequential builder walks its agents and adds a direct edge
between each adjacent pair --- it is the \code{AddChain} of
Chapter~\ref{ch:workflows-basics} with agents in it. The concurrent builder is
more revealing still: it calls \code{AddFanOutEdge} from a start node to the
agents, and \code{AddFanInBarrierEdge} from accumulators into an end node.

That is the entire mechanism. Fan-out for concurrency, a barrier for the join ---
exactly the two edge kinds of \S\ref{sec:wf-edges}, with the boilerplate written
for you.

\begin{note}
The consequence worth carrying: \textbf{anything a pattern can do, you can do with
the builder, and the reverse is not true}. The patterns are a vocabulary of five
common shapes. When your problem is one of those five, use the pattern --- it is
less code and less to get wrong. When it is not, you are not blocked, you are
just back in Chapter~\ref{ch:workflows-basics}. There is no cliff between the two,
which is the framework's best structural decision in this area.
\end{note}

You can see this for yourself with the visualiser from \S\ref{sec:wf-events},
which is the fastest way to make the point concrete:

\begin{csharp}[caption={A pattern is a graph}, label={lst:orch-visualise}]
Workflow workflow = AgentWorkflowBuilder.BuildConcurrent(researcher, analyst, critic);

// Prints the fan-out, the three agent nodes, and the barrier.
Console.WriteLine(workflow.ToMermaidString());
\end{csharp}

\needscreenshot{orchestration-pattern-as-graph}{The concurrent pattern is a
fan-out and a barrier}{Two rendered Mermaid diagrams side by side. On the left,
the graph a three-agent concurrent workflow produces. On the right, the same
graph hand-built with a fan-out edge and a barrier edge. The reader must be able
to compare them node for node, so keep all identifiers legible. No editor
chrome.}

The shared base contributes the parts every pattern needs --- naming, and marking
which agents' output counts:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}lX@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Member} & \textbf{Purpose} \\
\midrule
\code{WithName} & Names the workflow, for traces and visualisation \\
\code{WithDescription} & Describes it \\
\code{WithOutputFrom} & Which agents' output is workflow output \\
\code{WithIntermediateOutputFrom} & Emit output without ending the run \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\section{Sequential}
\label{sec:orch-sequential}
\index{orchestration!sequential}

The simplest pattern and the one to reach for by default. Agents run in order,
each seeing what came before.

\begin{csharp}[caption={A sequential pipeline}, label={lst:orch-sequential}]
Workflow workflow = AgentWorkflowBuilder.BuildSequential(
    outliner, drafter, editor, factChecker);
\end{csharp}

There is a builder form too, when you need to name the workflow or configure it.
One option is worth knowing about: by default the chain carries the whole
conversation forward, and it can be restricted to carrying only the agents'
responses. That is a token-cost decision, not a correctness one, and it is the
kind of thing \S\ref{sec:orch-benchmark} exists to settle.

\begin{note}
Sequential is underrated. A large fraction of what gets built as a group chat is
a sequence with extra steps and three times the token cost, and the giveaway is
that the ``discussion'' always proceeds in the same order anyway. If you can
write down the order in advance, it is a sequence.
\end{note}

\section{Concurrent}
\label{sec:orch-concurrent}
\index{orchestration!concurrent}

Several agents see the same input and work independently; their results are
gathered. This is the pattern with the clearest win, because the latency
improvement is structural rather than incidental --- three agents that each take
four seconds take four seconds together rather than twelve.

\begin{csharp}[caption={Independent analyses, joined}, label={lst:orch-concurrent}]
Workflow workflow = AgentWorkflowBuilder.CreateConcurrentBuilderWith(
        securityReviewer, performanceReviewer, styleReviewer)
    .WithAggregator(results => results.SelectMany(r => r).ToList())
    .WithName("code-review")
    .Build();
\end{csharp}

The aggregator is the part to think about. Its default concatenates; whether that
is what you want depends entirely on what happens next. Concatenation is right
when a human reads the output, and usually wrong when another agent does --- three
reviews stapled together are three times the context for a summariser that has to
reconcile them.

\begin{warning}
Concurrency multiplies cost even when it divides latency. Three agents on one
input is three times the input tokens, every time, whether or not all three had
anything useful to say. The pattern pays when the analyses are genuinely
independent and all of them are wanted. It does not pay as a way of ``getting a
second opinion'' on work that one agent already did adequately --- that is a
sequential critic, and it costs one extra call rather than $n$.
\end{warning}

\section{Group chat}
\label{sec:orch-groupchat}
\index{orchestration!group chat}

Agents take turns in a shared conversation, and a manager decides who speaks
next. The manager is the pattern; everything else is participants.

\begin{csharp}[caption={Round-robin turn taking}, label={lst:orch-groupchat}]
Workflow workflow = AgentWorkflowBuilder
    .CreateGroupChatBuilderWith(agents =>
        new RoundRobinGroupChatManager(agents) { MaximumIterationCount = 5 })
    .AddParticipants(french, spanish, english)
    .WithName("translation-round-robin")
    .Build();
\end{csharp}

\api{GroupChatManager} is abstract, and its one required member selects the next
agent. The in-box implementation rotates. Anything cleverer --- selecting by
model, by keyword, by whose turn produced the least progress --- is a subclass.

Two details of the base class matter in practice. It tracks an iteration count
and enforces a maximum, which is the difference between a group chat and a
non-terminating one. And it has its own checkpointing hooks with state keys
automatically prefixed, so that a manager's state is isolated from the host's ---
which means a custom manager that keeps state must persist it through those hooks
to survive a resume, exactly as \S\ref{sec:dur-hooks} described for executors.

\begin{warning}
\textbf{Set the iteration limit deliberately.} A group chat's failure mode is not
a crash, it is agents agreeing with each other at length until the limit is hit
and the run ends with no conclusion. The limit is the safety net, not the
termination condition --- if your chats routinely end by hitting it, the pattern
is not converging and adding iterations will not fix it.
\end{warning}

\begin{note}
Group chat is the pattern most often chosen for the wrong reason. It reads well in
a demo because the transcript looks like collaboration. Ask what the manager is
actually deciding: if the answer is ``it rotates'', you have a sequence in a loop
and should compare it against one before shipping it.
\end{note}

\section{Handoff}
\label{sec:orch-handoff}
\index{orchestration!handoff}

Handoff is transfer of control. One agent decides that another agent should take
over the conversation, and does. This pays off the line drawn in
Chapter~\ref{ch:tools}, \S\ref{sec:agents-as-tools}: exposing an agent as a
function is \emph{delegation}
--- the caller keeps control and gets a result back --- whereas handoff is
\emph{transfer}, and the first agent is no longer driving.

\subsection{Declaring the topology}

You declare who may hand off to whom. The triage-and-specialists shape is the
canonical one:

\begin{csharp}[caption={A handoff topology}, label={lst:orch-handoff}]
Workflow workflow = AgentWorkflowBuilder.CreateHandoffBuilderWith(triage)
    .WithHandoffs(triage, [mathTutor, historyTutor])
    .WithHandoffs([mathTutor, historyTutor], triage)
    .Build();
\end{csharp}

Adding participants without declaring any handoffs wires every agent to every
other agent. That is a convenient default for three agents and a poor one for
ten, because the number of transfer options the model must choose between grows
with the square of the participants.

\subsection{Injected transfer tools}

The mechanism is worth knowing because it explains both the pattern's
capabilities and its failure modes. Handoff is implemented by giving each agent
\emph{tools} --- one per permitted target, named with a common prefix. The model
transfers control by calling one.

Which means the model's choice of target is a tool-selection problem, and it is
made on the strength of the tool's description. That description is not something
you supply separately: the builder derives it from the target agent, falling back
through its description, then its name, then its instructions.

\begin{warning}
\textbf{An agent with no description, no name and no instructions cannot be a
handoff target.} The builder throws when it registers the handoff, which is the
right behaviour and an unusually good error --- but the more common problem is the
one that does not throw. An agent with a vague description is a valid target that
the model will route to badly. The description is not documentation here. It is
the routing table, read by a model, at run time.

Write descriptions for handoff targets as though they were the only thing
determining whether a user reaches the right specialist, because they are.
\end{warning}

Because transfers are tool calls, they appear in the conversation as tool calls,
which is usually not what you want the next agent to see. The builder therefore
offers filtering, with three settings:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}lX@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Setting} & \textbf{Effect} \\
\midrule
\code{None} & Agents see all tool calls, including handoff plumbing \\
\code{HandoffOnly} & Handoff calls are hidden; ordinary tool calls remain \\
\code{All} & No tool calls are visible to downstream agents \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\noindent
\code{HandoffOnly} is the setting most people want and not the default. Leaving
it unset means a specialist agent's context contains the mechanics of how it came
to be called, which is noise at best and an invitation to comment on the routing
at worst.

\subsection{The hard case: handing back mid-turn}

A specialist that finishes its work needs somewhere to go. Two mechanisms exist
and they are not equivalent.

The explicit one is to declare the reverse handoff, as the topology above does
--- specialists can hand back to triage. The model must then choose to do so,
which it may not, and a specialist that keeps the conversation is a specialist
answering questions outside its competence.

The other is to enable returning to the previous agent, which makes the handoff
stack-like rather than a free graph. That is a better fit for the
triage-and-return shape, because returning becomes structural rather than
another decision the model has to get right.

There is also an autonomous mode, in which agents continue without waiting for
user input, bounded by a turn limit and driven by a continuation prompt --- both
configurable globally or per agent. Treat the turn limit as a budget rather than
a safety net, and set it having thought about what a runaway costs.

\subsection{Topology to the developer, routing to the model}

That subheading is the design, and it is worth stating as a principle because it
tells you where to spend your effort.

\textbf{You own the topology.} Which transfers are possible is a static property
of your code, reviewable in a pull request, and enforceable. An agent cannot
transfer to an agent you did not connect. This is a real safety property and it
is the reason handoff is preferable to an agent that can invoke arbitrary other
agents.

\textbf{The model owns the routing.} Which transfer happens, on this input, is a
run-time decision made by a language model reading tool descriptions.

Debugging follows the split. A transfer that should have been possible and was
not is your bug, in the builder. A transfer that was possible and went to the
wrong place is a description problem, or an instructions problem, and no amount
of rewiring will fix it. Establishing which of the two you have --- by checking
whether the tool was even offered --- is the first step, and it takes thirty
seconds if you look at the trace.

\section{Magentic}
\label{sec:orch-magentic}
\index{orchestration!Magentic}

There is a fifth pattern, it is materially different from the other four, and it
is largely undocumented outside the source.

In the first four patterns, the control structure is fixed at build time: a
chain, a fan-out, a rotation, a topology. Magentic's is not. A manager agent
writes a plan, delegates to participants, evaluates progress after each round,
and replans when progress stops.

\begin{csharp}[caption={A planning manager}, label={lst:orch-magentic}]
Workflow workflow = new MagenticWorkflowBuilder(manager)
    .AddParticipants([researcher, coder])
    .WithMaxRounds(10)
    .WithMaxStalls(3)
    .WithMaxResets(2)
    .RequirePlanSignoff(false)
    .WithName("magentic-research")
    .Build();
\end{csharp}

The three limits are the interesting part, because they encode a model of how
this kind of orchestration fails. Rounds bound the total work. \emph{Stalls}
bound consecutive rounds in which no progress was made. \emph{Resets} bound how
many times the manager may throw away its plan and start again.

That vocabulary comes from a progress ledger the manager maintains, which tracks
whether the request is satisfied, whether the conversation is looping, whether
progress is being made, who should speak next, and what they should be asked.

\begin{note}
\textbf{Loop detection is the feature here.} Every other pattern's answer to
``these agents are going in circles'' is an iteration cap --- it stops the
bleeding without noticing the wound. Magentic distinguishes a round that made no
progress from a round that merely took time, and can respond by replanning rather
than by continuing to spend. Whether it detects this \emph{well} is an empirical
question this book does not yet have data on; that it attempts the distinction at
all is what makes it worth a section.
\end{note}

Plan sign-off is the other notable capability, and it is where this chapter meets
Chapter~\ref{ch:workflows-advanced}: requiring sign-off makes the manager's plan
subject to human approval before any work is done. That is a request port in the
sense of \S\ref{sec:dur-hitl}, which means a Magentic run awaiting plan approval
checkpoints and resumes like anything else --- an approval gate in front of an
expensive multi-agent run, for one configuration call.

The pattern also emits its own events, so a plan and its revisions can be
streamed rather than inferred:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}lX@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Event} & \textbf{Raised when} \\
\midrule
\code{MagenticPlanCreatedEvent} & The manager has produced its initial plan \\
\code{MagenticReplannedEvent} & The plan has been revised after a stall \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\begin{versionbox}
Magentic's prompts are English by default and overridable, with the built-in
templates exposed so they can be copied and translated rather than rewritten from
scratch. A response language can also be set directly. If you are building for a
non-English audience, this is the pattern that needs the most attention --- its
behaviour depends on prompts the framework supplies, not only on yours.
\end{versionbox}

\needscreenshot{magentic-plan-and-ledger}{A manager planning, stalling and
replanning}{Console output of a Magentic run with the plan-created and replanned
events printed, showing the initial plan, at least one round of delegation, and a
replan triggered by a stall. The progress-ledger fields --- whether progress is
being made, and the chosen next speaker --- must be visible for at least one
round. Plain terminal.}

\section{A benchmark across all five}
\label{sec:orch-benchmark}
\index{orchestration!benchmark}

Everything above is description. Choosing between the patterns needs numbers, and
the numbers do not exist publicly in any form worth citing. This section
specifies the experiment.

\begin{warning}
\textbf{This section reports no results.} The harness below is a design, not a
completed study. The tables in Appendix~\ref{app:providers} are deliberately
empty and will stay empty until the experiment has actually been run. Any
comparative statement elsewhere in this chapter is labelled as judgement, because
that is what it is.

A book that told you concurrent orchestration costs 2.8$\times$ the tokens of
sequential, without having measured it, would be worse than one that admits it
has not.
\end{warning}

\subsection{The design}

\textbf{One task, five implementations.} The task must be fixed, non-trivial, and
have a checkable answer --- otherwise success rate is unmeasurable and you are
comparing prose. A research-and-summarise task over a fixed document set works,
because correctness can be scored against known facts.

\textbf{Hold everything else constant.} Same model, same temperature, same agent
instructions, same tools. The only variable is the orchestration builder. This is
harder than it sounds: the patterns have different natural prompt shapes, and the
temptation to tune each one is the temptation to measure your tuning.

\textbf{Four metrics}, and all four are needed, because every pattern wins on at
least one:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}lX@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Metric} & \textbf{How} \\
\midrule
Turn count & Count agent invocations from executor events \\
Token cost & Sum usage content across the run, input and output separately \\
Latency & Wall clock, first input to final output \\
Success rate & Scored against the known answer, over $n$ runs \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\textbf{Report distributions, not best runs.} These systems do not repeat
themselves, so a single run of each pattern is an anecdote. Run each at least
twenty times and report the median and the spread. A pattern with a good median
and a terrible tail is not a good pattern, and reporting only the median hides
exactly the failure you care about.

\begin{csharp}[caption={The harness shape}, label={lst:orch-harness}]
foreach (var (name, workflow) in Patterns(agents))
{
    for (int run = 0; run < RunCount; run++)
    {
        Stopwatch sw = Stopwatch.StartNew();
        RunMetrics metrics = new(name, run);

        await using StreamingRun handle =
            await InProcessExecution.RunStreamingAsync(workflow, Task);

        await foreach (WorkflowEvent evt in handle.WatchStreamAsync())
        {
            switch (evt)
            {
                case ExecutorInvokedEvent: metrics.Turns++; break;
                case WorkflowOutputEvent output: metrics.Record(output); break;
            }
        }

        metrics.Elapsed = sw.Elapsed;
        metrics.Correct = await Scorer.ScoreAsync(metrics.Output, ExpectedAnswer);
        await results.WriteAsync(metrics);
    }
}
\end{csharp}

\begin{note}
Build the workflows through a factory keyed by pattern name, exactly as
Chapter~\ref{ch:workflows-advanced} required for resume. A benchmark that
constructs its graphs inline drifts between patterns without anyone noticing,
which is the most common way a comparison like this becomes worthless.
\end{note}

\subsection{What the experiment cannot tell you}

Being clear about this is what separates a benchmark from a marketing chart.

The result is specific to one task, one model and one set of instructions. It
does not generalise to your task. What generalises is the \emph{method}, and the
rough shape of the trade --- that concurrency buys latency with tokens, that
handoff buys specialisation with routing risk. The numbers are evidence for a
decision on this task; they are not a ranking of the patterns.

Nor does it measure quality beyond a binary correctness score. Two patterns can
both be correct and differ enormously in how useful the output is, which is a gap
Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} exists to close.

\begin{note}
Run this before Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} and keep the raw event streams, not
just the summary rows. Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} scores the same runs under a
proper evaluation harness, and Chapter~\ref{ch:capstone} compares topologies
empirically --- both need the traces, and re-running to get them is expensive.
\end{note}

\section{When to drop to raw executors and edges}
\label{sec:orch-dropping}

The patterns cover common shapes. Here are the signals that yours is not one of
them, in roughly the order they appear.

\textbf{You are fighting the aggregator.} Post-processing a pattern's output into
the shape you needed means the join is wrong, and the join is one line of graph.

\textbf{You need a non-agent step in the middle.} Validation, a database write, a
deterministic transform. The patterns take agents. The graph takes anything ---
which is the symmetry of \S\ref{sec:wf-function-executor}, and the point at which
it stops being theoretical.

\textbf{Your control flow is conditional on data.} Patterns route on model output
or a fixed structure. If the next step depends on a computed value, you want a
conditional edge.

\textbf{You need to pause somewhere specific.} Approval on one branch only, as in
\S\ref{sec:dur-pipeline}, is a request port on that edge --- not something a
pattern exposes. Magentic's plan sign-off is the exception that proves this: it
is a pattern with one pause built in, at one point.

\textbf{You are combining two patterns.} A concurrent fan-out whose results feed
a group chat is not a pattern, it is a graph with a fan-out and a group chat host
in it. Compose at the graph level.

\begin{note}
Dropping to the graph is not an escalation and it does not cost you the patterns.
An orchestration builder produces a \api{Workflow}, and a workflow can be a node
in another workflow. The realistic end state for a large system is a hand-built
graph with pattern-built subgraphs inside it --- not a choice between the two.
\end{note}

\begin{exercisebox}
Before Chapter~\ref{ch:observability}:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item Build the same three-agent review with \code{BuildConcurrent} and by hand
      with a fan-out and a barrier. Render both with
      \code{ToMermaidString()} and diff them. Whatever the diff shows is what the
      pattern was doing for you.
\item Run the benchmark in \S\ref{sec:orch-benchmark} for real, twenty runs per
      pattern, and fill in Appendix~\ref{app:providers}. This is the single most
      valuable thing in this book that has not been done yet.
\item Remove a handoff target's description and confirm the builder throws. Then
      make it merely vague and confirm that it does not --- and watch the routing
      degrade.
\item Give a group chat a task with no natural stopping point and watch it hit
      the iteration limit. Then implement a manager that terminates on agreement
      instead, and compare token cost.
\item Take the Magentic workflow, turn on plan sign-off, and reject the first
      plan. Confirm the run checkpoints while it waits, using
      Chapter~\ref{ch:workflows-advanced}'s machinery.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercisebox}

\section{Summary}

The orchestration patterns are builders that emit ordinary workflows. The
sequential builder adds direct edges between adjacent agents; the concurrent
builder adds a fan-out and a barrier. Reading those two \code{Build()} methods is
the fastest way to stop thinking of the patterns as a separate system, and it is
what tells you that leaving them is a step sideways rather than a step down.

Sequential is the default and is underrated. Concurrent divides latency and
multiplies cost, so it pays when the analyses are genuinely independent and all
of them are wanted. Group chat needs a manager that is deciding something --- if
it merely rotates, compare it against a sequence before shipping it --- and its
iteration limit is a safety net rather than a termination condition.

Handoff is transfer rather than delegation, implemented by injecting one tool per
permitted target. The topology is yours and is enforceable; the routing is the
model's and is made by reading the target agents' descriptions, which makes those
descriptions a routing table rather than documentation. Filter handoff tool calls
out of downstream context, and prefer structural return over hoping the model
chooses to hand back.

Magentic is a fifth pattern with a different character: a manager that plans,
delegates, evaluates progress and replans, bounded by rounds, stalls and resets.
Stall detection is the capability the other four lack, and optional plan sign-off
puts a human approval gate in front of the work using
Chapter~\ref{ch:workflows-advanced}'s machinery.

Choosing between them needs measurement, and this chapter specifies the
experiment rather than reporting it: one fixed task with a checkable answer, five
implementations with everything else held constant, four metrics, twenty runs
each, distributions rather than best runs. The tables in
Appendix~\ref{app:providers} stay empty until that is done.

Chapter~\ref{ch:observability} makes the runs visible, which is what turns the
benchmark from a spreadsheet into an explanation.
